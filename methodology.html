Project Methodology

Understanding my research process, data decisions, and analytical framework

Research Questions

This project is guided by three central questions:

  • How did textile handicraft traditions vary across ethnolinguistic groups and geographic regions in Nigeria?
  • How did British colonialism and foreign imports specifically disrupt traditional knowledge systems and women's labor practices?
  • Can digital mapping effectively visualize colonial economic disruption as a process rather than a singular event?

These questions drive my data collection, geospatial analysis, and interpretive framework, centering women's expertise and labor within broader colonial histories.

Sources & Data

My data is drawn from multiple archival and scholarly sources:

Archival Materials
  • British Colonial Office papers (National Archives, UK)
  • Missionary records and photographs (Rhodes House Library, Oxford)
  • Trade reports and economic surveys
Museum Collections
  • British Museum Nigerian textile collections
  • Smithsonian National Museum of African Art
  • Field Museum, Chicago
Scholarly Sources
  • Published ethnographic studies
  • Contemporary historical analyses
  • Oral histories collected by Nigerian scholars
Community Contributions

The Community Spotlight feature invites contemporary practitioners, cultural heritage keepers, and community members to share their connections to textile traditions. These submissions serve as ethnographic documentation of living practices and help balance the historical archive's colonial perspective.

Critical Approach: I read colonial archives "against the grain," centering women's expertise despite archival bias and seeking voices that colonial documentation marginalized or dismissed.

Analytical Approach

My methodology combines spatial humanities, feminist historiography, and postcolonial analysis:

Geospatial Mapping

I use Leaflet.js and GeoJSON to map textile traditions across geographic regions, connecting material culture to specific communities and ethnolinguistic groups. Historical maps are georeferenced using QGIS to provide geographic context.

Temporal Analysis

The project documents change across time periods (1850–1885, 1885–1920, 1920–1960), showing how colonial policies transformed production patterns over time rather than presenting colonialism as a singular disruptive event. Time periods are recorded in the data and displayed contextually rather than used to filter markers — a deliberate methodological decision to avoid imposing rigid chronological boundaries on traditions that are continuous and fluid by nature.

Centering Women's Labor

I foreground women's expertise, economic contributions, and knowledge systems that colonial archives often marginalized. Each location entry documents both the craft tradition and its practitioners, drawing on feminist scholarship including Amadiume's Male Daughters, Female Husbands (1987), Byfield's The Bluest Hands (2002), and Kriger's work on gender and textile production in the Sokoto Caliphate (1993).

Limitations

I acknowledge several limitations in my methodology and data:

Archival Gaps
  • Colonial archives privilege European perspectives and male actors
  • Many regions and time periods lack comprehensive documentation
  • Women's voices are often mediated through colonial observers
Georeferencing Challenges
  • Historical place names don't always correspond to precise modern coordinates
  • Ethnic territories were fluid and contested, not fixed boundaries
  • Some craft traditions were practiced across wide geographic areas
Temporal Uncertainty
  • Not all sources provide exact dates for craft practices
  • Change occurred at different rates in different regions
  • Some traditions persisted or revived in ways difficult to document
Transparency: Where data is incomplete or uncertain, I document these gaps explicitly rather than making unsubstantiated claims. My goal is to present the most accurate picture possible while acknowledging the constraints of the historical record.
